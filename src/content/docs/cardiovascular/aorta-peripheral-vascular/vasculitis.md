## Large vessel vasculitis

### Takayasu disease

**Aetiology of Takayasu arteritis**

- Populations with high TB prevalence appear to have greater incidence of TAK
- Does not explain incidence in Japan

**Pathology**

- Necrotising inflammatory disease of large and medium sized vessels
- First affects tunica adventitia but eventually causes transmural involvement
- Can involve from carotid till renal arteries
    - Commonly subclavian (L > R), arch and CCA
    - Pulmonary arteries 30%
    - Coronary arteries 10%
- Hata classification:
    - type I: the branches from aortic arch
    - type II:
        - IIa: the ascending aorta, the aortic arch and its branches
        - IIb: the ascending aorta, aortic arch and its branches and thoracic descending aorta
    - type III: the thoracic descending aorta, abdominal aorta and/or renal arteries
    - type IV: the abdominal aorta and/or the renal arteries
    - type V: combination of IIb and IV

**Clinical features**
- Marked female predilection (9:1) and in young ages < 50 years
- Clinical features significant for diagnosis: ACR/EULAR (2022) (total &ge; 5 suggests TAK):
    - Essential criteria: age <60 and vasculitis on imaging
    - Clinical criteria:
        - Female sex     +1
        - Angina or ischemic cardiac pain     +2
        - Arm or leg claudication     +2
        - Vascular bruit     +2
        - Reduced pulse in upper extremity     +2
        - Carotid artery abnormality     +2
        - Systolic blood pressure difference in arms ≥20 mmHg     +1
    - Imaging criteria:
        - number of affected arterial territories (+1 to +3)
        - thoracic aorta, abdominal aorta, mesenteric, left or right carotid, left or right subclavian, left or right renal arteries
        - paired artery involvement (+1) - subclavian, carotid, renal
        - abdominal aorta plus renal or mesenteric involvement (+3).
- Other diagnostic criteria in use: Sharma (1995), Ishikawa's criteria

**Imaging modalities**

- USG: initial for superficial arteries (carotid, abdominal aorta, renal)
    - Macaroni sign: echogenic symmetrically thickened carotid artery walls
- MRA vs CTA (EULAR recommends MRA as 1st line):
    - Better contrast resolution of vessel wall enhancement for disease activity
    - Lack of ionising radiation in young patients who need repeat imaging for disease activity
- PET-CT: good at detection of early disease (prestenotic inflammatory phase)
- DSA: used to be gold standard for lumen but now fallen out of favour as cannot evaluate walls

**MRI in Takayasu disease**

- Institutional protocol:
    - Non-contrast MR angiography (optional if CE-MRA available):
        - TOF for head and neck
        - bSSFP for chest and abdomen (Siemens: TruFISP, GE: FIESTA, Philips: BTFE)
        - bSSP with background suppression for renal arteries (Siemens: NATIVE-TruFISP, GE: Inhance Inflow IR, Philips: B-TRANCE)
    - Pre- and post-contrast vessel wall imaging (3D T1w TSE sequences, e.g., SPACE, CUBE, VISTA) in axial and oblique sagittal (for aorta)
    - 3 station contrast MR angiography 
    - 3 station T2w black blood imaging with double IR for flow suppression
- Findings: luminal irregularity, vessel stenosis, occlusion, dilatation or aneurysms in the aorta or its primary branches
    - Aneurysms in 2-6%, aneurysms without stenosis in 1-2%
    - Vessel dissection is extremely rare
- Imaging findings of active disease
    - MRI: mural enhancement on post-contrast vessel wall imaging with intramural oedema on T2 weighted black blood sequences, DWI has promising role
    - CT: double ring sign (inner low attenuating wall surrounded by an outer high attenuating wall, may be visible in the venous phase)
    - PET-CT: ↑ vessel wall SUV
    - Clinically, raised ESR is suspicious for active disease

**Differential diagnosis**
:::differentials
| Category | GCA | TAK |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Age | >50 y |	<40 y |
| Sex (F:M) | 3:1 | 8–9:1 |
| Histologic findings | Medial inflammation and necrosis | Intimal and adventitial fibrosis |
| Disease course | Shorter and less severe flares | Longer and more severe flares with complications |
| Pattern of involvement | Symmetric<br>Temporal artery involvement +++<br>Axillary artery involvement ++<br>Vertebrobasilar artery involvement ++ | Asymmetric<br>Subclavian (left > right) and common carotid artery involvement +++<br>Visceral artery involvement ++<br>No temporal artery involvement |
| Complications | Visual disturbance and loss<br>Aneurysm and dissection | Luminal stenosis, occlusions<br>Renovascular hypertension<br>Heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, myocardial infarction |
| Associated conditions | Polymyalgia rheumatica |Inflammatory bowel disease<br>Sarcoidosis<br>Ankylosing spondylitis |
:::

**Treatment**
- Medical: Adalimumab/tocilizumab/methotrexate/azathioprine/mycophenolate mofetil + medium/high dose glucocorticoids
- Interventional: not done in active disease due to high restenosis rate unless life threatening:
    - Uncontrolled hypertension with 70% renal artery stenosis (pressure gradient > 20 mmHg)
    - Clinically manifest mesenteric ischaemia with focal, haemodynamically significant mesenteric artery stenosis
- Interventions in inactive disease:
    - Inactive disease with a normal ESR < 20 mm/hr
    - Stable IMT in the carotid artery
    - Stenting: if flow limiting dissection, persistent severe stenosis after balloon angioplasty
        - Makes repeat angioplasty difficult in restenosis
    - Successful angioplasty: 
        - < 30% residual stenosis
        - 50% larger than prestenotic caliber
        - Gradient < 20 mmHg
    - Surgery: long segment disease or aneurysms - bypass
